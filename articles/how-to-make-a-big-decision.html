How to make a big decision when you can't stop overthinking.

By Luke Haythorpe · 7 min read

You've made the pros and cons list. Twice. You've asked everyone you know, and now their opinions are just more noise. The decision is still sitting there, unmade, living rent-free in your head every day. You probably have enough information already. More of it stopped helping a while ago.

Overthinking feels responsible. You're gathering facts, weighing things up carefully. But after a certain point, more analysis becomes a way of putting off the discomfort of choosing. You go round the same options without getting any closer.

Here's how to break the loop by giving your brain a more useful job.

Why your brain is stuck on repeat

When you're overthinking a decision, one of these is usually going on underneath:

You're trying to eliminate all risk. Part of you believes that if you think hard enough, you'll find the option with no downside. It doesn't exist. Every real choice costs you the option you didn't pick, and endless analysis is often a way of not accepting that.

You're waiting to feel sure. You want to be 100% certain before you move. But certainty usually comes after you decide. You're waiting for a feeling that only shows up once you've already jumped.

You're afraid of owning the outcome. If you never quite decide, nobody can quite blame you, including you. Staying in the loop lets you avoid responsibility. It's uncomfortable to hear, but worth checking.

Most of the time you have enough information. You're waiting to feel certain, and certainty almost always shows up after the decision.

Get your gut and your reasoning in the same room

Most overthinkers lean too hard on one side. Some have analysed the thing to death without checking what their gut already knows. Others have a strong instinct but won't trust it until they've justified it a hundred times. Use both, on purpose.

The gut check

Imagine you've decided. Pick one option, commit to it in your mind, and live with it for a day as if it's settled. Notice how your body reacts. Relief? Dread? A small "yes"? Your instinct often gets there before your logic does, as long as you give it a concrete scenario to react to.

The reasoning check

Then use logic to test your gut rather than overrule it. If your instinct says yes, ask what would have to be true for this to be a good decision, and whether those things are true. If your gut says no, ask whether you're reacting to the option itself or just to the fear of change.

Practical ways to break the loop

Set a deadline. A decision with no deadline can drag on forever. Give yourself a real date: "I'll decide by Friday." Having a cut-off makes your mind land somewhere.

Lower the stakes in your head. Most decisions feel permanent and aren't. Ask yourself: can I undo this? Can I adjust later? Usually you can, so the decision is a lot less final than your anxiety is telling you.

Think about what you'd tell a friend. If a friend described your exact situation, what would you say? We're usually clearer about other people's decisions because we're not caught up in the fear. Use that clarity on yourself.

Ask what you'd regret. Fear keeps you focused on what each option costs. Regret is a better guide. Picture yourself at 40 looking back. Which choice would you regret not making? That question clears a lot of noise quickly.

When you need another head

Sometimes you've run out of your own perspective, and more thinking on your own won't help. You're stuck in a loop, and it's very hard to think your way out of a loop from inside it.

This is one of the most practical reasons people work with a coach. I won't tell you what to decide, because that wouldn't help and wouldn't stick. I'll ask good questions, play your reasoning back to you, and keep you steady while you reach a conclusion. One good conversation can undo a loop you've been stuck in for months, because someone on the outside can see what you can't.

Past a certain point, overthinking is mostly fear dressed up as being careful. To get out, use your gut and your reasoning together, set a real deadline, and accept that no choice is risk-free. Decide, and let certainty catch up. It will.
