How do I get unstuck in my career?

By Luke Haythorpe · 7 min read

You know you want something different. You just can't name it. You've read the advice, taken the quizzes, made the lists, and somehow everything sounds possible and nothing feels possible. So you stay where you are, a little more tired each month.

If that's you, here's the first thing I'd want you to hear: you don't need to know your next career to start moving. Most people who change direction well didn't begin with an answer. They began with a better question, and one small way to find something out.

Being stuck usually isn't a lack of ideas. It's that every idea feels like a giant, all-or-nothing bet, so none of them get tested. The way out is to make the next step smaller than the fear.

Start with what you have to work around

Career advice loves to skip this part. Before you dream, name the real limits. How much income do you need to keep? How many hours a week can you actually give to this? Is there family, health, or a location you're working around?

This isn't about shrinking the dream. It's about choosing a next step that fits your actual life, so you'll take it. A plan that needs you to quit next month or retrain full time isn't a plan if you can't do either. Two hours a week is a real starting point. So is one lunch break.

Look at tasks, not titles

When you're stuck, you tend to think in job titles. Teacher. Designer. Something in tech. Titles are too big and too vague to tell you much.

Instead, look at your current work, even the job you want to leave, and notice which tasks give you energy. Explaining something until it clicks for someone. Untangling a messy process. Calming a frustrated customer. Making something tidy and finished. Those are clues. They travel with you into all kinds of work.

The job you want to leave is still full of information about what you'd want next.

Name two things to look into

Using those tasks as a clue, name two kinds of work you're curious about. Not one, because one becomes a verdict. Not ten, because ten becomes another list. Two.

Treat them as guesses to test, not decisions to defend. You're not choosing your future here. You're choosing what to learn about next. If someone who loves helping new starters find their feet is curious about training roles and about team leadership, that's two good guesses. They might both turn out to be wrong. That's still progress.

Find out, before you decide

Here's where most people stall. They go back to thinking. Thinking feels productive, and it's safe, but it rarely gives you anything new to work with.

What moves you is information you don't have yet. Pick one small way to get some:

  • Talk to someone who does the work. Twenty minutes over coffee or a call. Ask what an ordinary Tuesday looks like, what surprised them, and how they got in.
  • Read three real job descriptions. Not to apply. Just to see what the work actually asks for, and what you already have.
  • Try a small version. Offer to run one training session at your current job. Help a friend with the thing. Volunteer for an afternoon.

Then give it a day. "By Thursday I'll message two people" is a step. "Sometime I should network" is a wish.

Look back, then take the next one

After you've taken the step, notice what you learned. Did the conversation make you lean in or quietly lose interest? Did the job descriptions feel like you, or like a costume? Either answer is useful. You've just turned a vague possibility into something you actually know.

Do that a few times and something shifts. You stop asking "what should I do with my life?" and start asking smaller, answerable questions. That's what unstuck feels like. It's less like a lightning bolt and more like walking.

When it's more urgent than that

If you've just lost your job, or money is running out and you need work within weeks, this slower kind of exploring might feel like a luxury. That's fair. When the pressure is that real, please don't carry it alone. Talk to someone who can help you think clearly and practically, whether that's a coach, a trusted mentor, or local employment support. Clear thinking is worth even more when time is short.

You don't have to know where you're going to take the next step. You only need to know what you want to find out. Start there, keep it small, and let the answer come from what you learn rather than what you fear.
